The Man the County Is Named For Died 159 Years Ago This Week

George Wilkins Kendall died at his ranch of "congestive chills" on October 21, 1867, at the age of 58, according to the Boerne Star. He was buried in Boerne Cemetery, where his is the only gravesite with a Texas Historical Commission marker. The county that bears his name had been created on January 10, 1862, while he was still living here.

Kendall was a native of New Hampshire who learned printing as a young man. With Francis A. Lumsden, he founded the New Orleans Picayune in 1837. The paper sold for a picayune, a 6 1/4-cent Spanish coin.

In 1841, Kendall joined the Texan Santa Fe Expedition as a reporter. The expedition was captured, and he was marched to Mexico City and imprisoned for about a year. He later wrote a book about the expedition from memory, and it sold 40,000 copies.

During the Mexican-American War, Kendall covered the fighting from Monterrey to Mexico City, using his own courier system, and became known as the first modern war correspondent.

Kendall moved to this area in 1857 and became a sheep rancher. He had brought 24 Spanish merino rams and churro ewes to the Nueces in 1852, and moved his flock to Waco Springs and Post Oak Springs near Boerne. He was profiting from sheep by 1856, and his flock grew to 3,500 within two years. He wrote about sheep for the Texas Almanac beginning in 1858, and built the first large dipping vats against the scab epidemic of 1864, when his flock numbered 5,000.

In February 1861, Kendall and his family took up residence at Post Oak Spring Ranch. Less than a year later, Kendall County was created on January 10, 1862. The first county tax records show he held 6,000 acres in the new county, worth over $42,000.

In 1859, several newspapers and the German residents of Boerne and New Braunfels launched a campaign to elect Kendall governor. He declined. As the Boerne Star recounts, Kendall wrote to the San Antonio Herald, "I have no taste for the calling of a politician, have never been in the business, and am too old to learn a new trade."

Kendall took part in his new county's civic life. The county commissioners selected him as a grand juror on May 18, 1863, and as one of a three-member board of school examiners.

His grave in Boerne Cemetery carries Texas Historical Commission Marker No. 2169, recorded in 1989. The marker stands at the northwest corner of the cemetery at the stone wall, on School Street and Main. Its inscription notes that Kendall was a native of New Hampshire, co-founded the New Orleans Picayune in 1837, moved to this area in 1857, became a sheep rancher, and that the county was named for him in 1862.

Kendall's grave stands on the same ground as the annual Gone But Not Forgotten Tour, which walks Boerne Cemetery on October 31. This year's tour starts at the east gate on School Street at 10:15, 10:45, and 11:15 a.m., and features eight former residents portrayed by actors in period dress. Kendall is not one of the eight portrayed this year.
